Abstract. Public infrastructure portals predominantly display civil works
in unstructured tabular lists, obscuring geographic context and inducing
cognitive confusion between calendar time elapsed, physical completion,
and reporting recency. This pilot study investigates whether an integrated map-and-timeline interface significantly enhances citizen comprehension compared to a list-based format. Utilizing CivicTrack, an infrastructure monitoring platform for Lamongan Regency, Indonesia, we
benchmarked 20 municipal projects across a three-phase empirical evaluation. Study 1 validated baseline data integrity. Study 2 executed a counterbalanced within-subjects crossover experiment (N = 16) across four
core retrieval tasks. Study 3 evaluated user interpretation of stale and
missing progress data. The results reveal a task-dependent dichotomy. Interactive geospatial maps significantly accelerated spatial retrieval (median 14.30 s vs. 33.30 s; paired t(15) = 18.48, p < 0.001), accompanied by
a descriptive accuracy gain from 81.2% (13/16) to 100.0% (16/16) (exact
McNemar p = 0.250). For schedule delay detection, chronological timelines significantly reduced completion time (median 27.75 s vs. 44.50 s;
paired t(15) = 16.79, p < 0.001), elevating delay identification accuracy
from 50.0% (8/16) to 93.8% (15/16) (exact McNemar p = 0.016) by mitigating the duration-work equivalence heuristic. Conversely, for isolated
attribute lookup, tabular lists were significantly faster (median 12.05 s
vs. 22.70 s; paired t(15) = −14.16, p < 0.001), with descriptive accuracy
favoring the list format (93.8% vs. 81.2%; exact McNemar p = 0.625),
exposing multi-view interaction overhead. In missing data evaluations,
31.2% misattributed missing values to project cancellation. Civic platforms must couple spatial overviews with frictionless tabular inspection.
Keywords: Civic Technology · Public Dashboard · Geospatial Visualization · Usability Evaluation · Open Data.

Introduction

Civic engagement in public infrastructure governance has emerged as an indispensable cornerstone of transparent, accountable, and participatory regional development [2, 5]. Across decentralized administrations and emerging economies,

2

municipal governments allocate substantial capital budgets to critical public
works—such as road resurfacing, flood mitigation canals, bridge renewals, and
primary healthcare centers [12]. Ordinary citizens have a vital and immediate
stake in these civil works, requiring accessible information to navigate daily commuting routes, evaluate public service delivery, and monitor whether municipal
tax expenditures translate into tangible community assets [13,16]. However, citizens seeking to discover local projects and track their execution progress confront
pervasive information barriers [2, 13].
In regional administrations such as Lamongan Regency, East Java, statutory
disclosures published via municipal e-procurement portals (e.g., LPSE Kabupaten Lamongan) and open government data repositories (e.g., Satu Data Lamongan) sequester public works data in static tabular registries, fragmented
spreadsheets, or dense PDF tender documents [12,16]. These legacy tabular formats strip away geographic context and spatial proximity, forcing citizens to
manually sift through hundreds of uncoordinated textual rows without an intuitive means to discern which construction projects impact their immediate
neighborhood or whether work has legitimately commenced on the ground.
A more acute cognitive and usability bottleneck identified during preliminary field observations is the prevalent conflation among three distinct operational dimensions: physical milestone progress, calendar duration elapsed, and
field reporting recency [13,16]. Preliminary field observations conducted between
August and September 2024 involved 12 civic community members and local
residents in Lamongan examining municipal public works information on LPSE
Kabupaten Lamongan and Satu Data Lamongan, the two primary official disclosure platforms referenced above. These observations were conducted as semistructured, task-based sessions, in which participants were asked to locate and
interpret information on a specific ongoing project while researchers recorded
their verbal reasoning and navigation behavior. These sessions revealed two severe cognitive breakdowns:
1. Duration-as-Progress Fallacy: Nine out of 12 participants erroneously
assumed that elapsed calendar time represented physical progress. When inspecting an ongoing road project showing “75% elapsed duration” alongside
“60% physical progress,” participants intuitively concluded that the project
was on track, failing to recognize a critical 15% execution delay [4].
2. Ambiguity over Stale and Missing Data: Official disclosures lacked
visual freshness indicators. When progress updates lagged by 3–5 months,
citizens could not discern whether projects were actively ongoing, quietly
halted, or completed; missing progress fields were frequently assumed to
denote project cancellation or 0% work [11].
To overcome the communicative limitations of raw spreadsheets, municipal
agencies and smart city platforms have increasingly transitioned toward visual
dashboards featuring interactive geographic maps and graphical charts [9, 15].
Nonetheless, existing civic dashboard implementations have predominantly been
driven by superficial interface aesthetics (“dashboard gloss”) rather than rigorous

human-computer interaction (HCI) evaluation grounded in user cognitive comprehension tasks [5, 6]. Current literature in open government data frequently
evaluates portals through technical interoperability, metadata completeness, or

actual information processing [10, 13]. Deploying rich cartographic layers and

duration from physical milestone progress with clear reporting recency indicators), and a structured, filterable list view (for direct attribute retrieval). We

interface significantly improve citizen accuracy and efficiency in understanding
project locations, physical progress, and schedule delays compared to a conventional list-based presentation?

2.1

Public Project Dashboards and Citizen Comprehension

Digital transparency portals are widely heralded as mechanisms to mitigate
information asymmetry between public agencies and citizens [2, 5, 8]. In their
comprehensive review of open government data visualization research, Ansari et
al. [2] emphasized that raw disclosure fails to achieve civic utility unless accompanied by task-tailored analytical tools that prevent cognitive overload. Matheus et
al. [12] formulated design principles for digital transparency in smart governance,
noting that portals must support structured inquiry rather than passive data exposure. In their user-centred evaluation of 41 national portals, Nikiforova and
McBride [13] revealed that most municipal implementations remain restricted
to passive “data dumps,” offering static tables that fail to support analytical
inquiry by lay citizens.
Chokki et al. [5] conducted controlled experiments demonstrating that interactive dashboards significantly enhance citizen comprehension compared to
isolated charts; however, they noted that multi-component dashboards trigger visual fragmentation if navigational hierarchies are ambiguous. Furthermore, Zhao
and Fan [16] and Kempeneer and Wolswinkel [8] observed that while citizen demand for local government data is high, persistent usability barriers frequently
discourage ongoing citizen monitoring. Crucially, computer-mediated presentations must provide transparent task-oriented structures, as disorienting visual
complexity can actively diminish perceived administrative competence.
2.2

Cognitive Fit Theory and Visualization Usability

The theoretical foundation explaining how representation formats interact with
task types is grounded in Cognitive Fit Theory, recently extended and evaluated
in multi-view data visualization environments by Bačić and Henry [4]. Under
this framework, problem-solving performance is optimized when the representation of information matches the cognitive task: spatial tasks demand perceptual,
holistic graphical displays, whereas symbolic, discrete lookups are best supported
by structured alphanumeric tables [4, 15]. In the visualization literature, Zytek
et al. [17] and Soni et al. [15] analyzed dashboard design spaces and multi-view
coordination, demonstrating that combining multiple visualization modalities introduces cognitive switching costs when users must coordinate views. Standardized usability evaluation protocols, such as those demonstrated by Heitkemper
et al. [6], provide validated instruments for quantifying user interaction friction
across comparative modalities.
2.3

Geospatial Visualization versus Tabular List Presentations

In the spatial domain, cartographic and geographic information systems (GIS)
research has rigorously analyzed the performance trade-offs between cartographic

maps and structured alphanumeric tables. In the spatial visualization literature,
recent empirical studies categorize visual analysis into elementary tasks (locating specific attribute values for an individual known entity) and synoptic tasks
(discerning spatial clusters, spatial relations, and regional distributions) [9, 10].
This theoretical framework posits that spatial visual representations are optimized for synoptic synthesis, whereas tabular lists minimize cognitive retrieval
costs for discrete elementary lookups.
This dichotomy was corroborated empirically by Maceli and Yu [11], whose
comparative usability evaluation reported that while interactive map interfaces
accelerated spatial pattern recognition and regional scoping relative to tabular formats, structured spreadsheet tables yielded superior accuracy and shorter
completion times when participants extracted exact quantitative attributes. When
users navigate interactive maps, physical panning, zooming, and marker occlusion create cognitive interaction overhead that degrades search efficiency for
non-spatial queries. In the context of participatory urban governance, Lehto
et al. [10], Kyttä et al. [9], and Johnson et al. [7] demonstrated that while spatial maps democratize geographic exploration, non-expert citizens struggle when
complex thematic layers compete for visual attention. Consequently, visual representations that seduce users with cartographic sophistication risk obscuring
underlying administrative data.

2.4

Timeline Usability and Schedule Representation

Visualizing chronological project progress requires representing durations, dependencies, and temporal milestones. Human working memory struggles to reconstruct temporal overlaps and pacing from raw alphanumeric date columns
in spreadsheets [15]. In project scheduling, Ayalew [3] evaluated deterministic
and fuzzy Earned Value Management (EVM) methods for public infrastructure
projects, underscoring that measuring physical progress against planned schedule baselines requires explicit variance tracking rather than simplistic duration
comparisons.

2.5

Interpretation of Missing and Stale Governance Data

A persistent challenge in public infrastructure monitoring is data latency and
reporting gaps. Visual displays that omit explicit visual indicators for missing
data frequently lead lay citizens to make negative default inferences, interpreting missing data as administrative failure or canceled works [11]. In civil infrastructure governance, Ambituuni [1] and Saelawong et al. [14] investigated the
Infrastructure Transparency Initiative (CoST) through principal-agent theory,
emphasizing that publishing open data is an institutional enabler rather than
an automatic remedy; without reliable, timely data, public disclosures fail to
generate accountability.

2.6

Research Gap and Positioning

Collectively, prior work treats these three representational paradigms in isolation: dashboard usability studies [2,5,12,13] evaluate aggregate interface comprehension without decomposing task type; map-versus-table studies [9–11] quantify
spatial-versus-tabular trade-offs but omit temporal schedule reasoning; and timeline visualization research [3,15] addresses duration representation without linking it to spatial or tabular retrieval. No prior study empirically compares all three
representations—map, timeline, and table—within a single counterbalanced task
battery on real municipal infrastructure data, nor explicitly measures how citizens misinterpret stale or missing progress fields. This paper addresses that
gap by integrating Cognitive Fit Theory [4] with a task-differentiated, withinsubjects evaluation of CivicTrack.

Methodology

3.1

Research Question and Differentiated Hypotheses

This study investigates the following primary research question: Does an integrated map-and-timeline interface improve citizen accuracy and efficiency in
understanding project locations, physical progress, and schedule delays compared
to a conventional list-based presentation?
Because completion time (continuous ratio variable, seconds) and comprehension accuracy (paired binary outcome, 0/1) represent distinct operational
dimensions evaluated under separate statistical tests (paired t-tests and exact
McNemar tests, respectively), we formulate separated operational hypotheses:
– H1 (Spatial Scoping): For spatial discovery tasks, the map interface will
achieve significantly shorter completion times (H1a ) and higher descriptive
or statistical retrieval accuracy (H1b ) by eliminating serial scanning fatigue.
– H2 (Schedule Pacing): For schedule delay detection, the dual-bar timeline interface will yield significantly faster completion times (H2a ) and significantly higher delay identification accuracy (H2b ) by mitigating the durationwork equivalence heuristic.
– H3 (Discrete Lookups): For discrete metadata lookups (latest field update
date), the tabular list will achieve significantly faster response times (H3a )
and superior descriptive accuracy (H3b ) due to the absence of multi-view
spatial navigation overhead.

3.5

Experimental Protocols

Study 1: Baseline Information Validation (Systemic Audit): Prior to
user testing, an automated verification script audited all 20 benchmark records,
confirming 100% data fidelity across backend APIs and frontend presentation
layers, ensuring no logic discrepancies between conditions.
Study 2: Controlled Comprehension Experiment (Pilot Study): Sixteen
participants (N = 16; 8 male, 8 female; aged 21–29 years, M = 24.2, SD = 2.6)
were recruited representing target civic users with varied educational and digital
literacy backgrounds. Testing was conducted in a controlled laboratory setting on
identical 15.6-inch Full HD (1920×1080) laptops running Google Chrome v128
with external optical mice. Participants completed a 10-minute standardized
familiarization tutorial using two dummy projects before formal testing.
A counterbalanced within-subjects crossover design (2 × 2 × 4) was deployed:
Group 1 (n = 8) performed Tasks 1–4 on Condition A (Set A) then Condition
B (Set B); Group 2 (n = 8) performed Tasks 1–4 on Condition B (Set A) then
Condition A (Set B). Methodological Note: While counterbalancing reduces order
and fatigue effects, it cannot guarantee their complete elimination.
The experimental tasks evaluated core civic scenarios with balanced target
questions:
– Task 1 (Location Retrieval): Set A: “Identify all public infrastructure
projects currently situated within Kecamatan Karangbinangun” (Key: Project
13, KBN-KUR-001). Set B: “Identify all public infrastructure projects situated
in Kecamatan Lamongan” (Key: Project 14, LMG-BNJ-001).
– Task 2 (Schedule Delay Identification): “Identify which project in the
assigned set is experiencing an execution delay relative to its scheduled milestone target.” (Set A Key: SGO-GND-001; Set B Key: SMB-RDR-001).
– Task 3 (Update Recency Lookup): “State the calendar date of the latest field inspection update for Project [Code] and verify whether this date

Study 3: Missing and Stale Data Perception Test: Immediately following
Study 2, participants evaluated three edge cases: (1) Case 1 (KTG-PCN-001):

84.8%; correct interpretation: unverified/missing; construction status cannot be

68.0%, update 117 days ago (Telapsed = 81.1%; correct interpretation: 68.0%
represents June progress; current October status is uncertain); and (3) Case
3 (KGN-KRN-001): Progress 55.0% reported on 15 June 2024, 122 days prior
(Tref = 15 October 2024, Telapsed = 74.8%; correct interpretation: On 15 June,
elapsed time was 34.7%, meaning the project was ahead in June; however, its
October status is unverifiable without current inspection reports).

Spatial Processing vs. Tabular Scanning (Task 1): Task 1 strongly corroborated hypothesis H1a : the interactive map significantly accelerated spatial

project retrieval by 57% (median 14.30 s vs. 33.30 s; paired t(15) = 18.48, p <
0.001). For retrieval accuracy (H1b ), Condition B achieved a descriptive gain
from 81.2% (13/16) to 100.0% (16/16). Because accuracy represents paired binary data, an exact two-sided McNemar test was evaluated across discordant
pairs (b = 0, c = 3), yielding p = 0.250. While choropleth visual clustering
eliminated serial scanning slips, the accuracy increase remains a descriptive improvement and does not reach statistical significance within this pilot cohort
(N = 16).

Mitigation of Schedule Delay Heuristics (Tasks 2 and 4): Tasks 2 and 4
supported hypotheses H2a and H2b , showing statistically verified gains in both
completion time and comprehension accuracy. For task efficiency (H2a ), the dualbar timeline significantly cut completion duration by 15–17 s (Task 2: paired
t(15) = 16.79, p < 0.001; Task 4: paired t(15) = 16.82, p < 0.001). Crucially, the
paired binary accuracy gains were statistically confirmed via exact McNemar
tests (H2b ): delay detection accuracy rose significantly from 50.0% (8/16) to
93.8% (15/16) in Task 2 (p = 0.016; discordant pairs b = 0, c = 7), and from
43.8% (7/16) to 93.8% (15/16) in Task 4 (p = 0.008; discordant pairs b = 0, c =
8). In the list condition, participants repeatedly fell victim to the duration-work
equivalence heuristic, misinterpreting high elapsed time as satisfactory execution.
The dual timeline neutralized this bias.

Interface Complexity Overhead in Elementary Lookups (Task 3): Importantly, Task 3 corroborated H3a , exposing the operational tax of spatial visual
interfaces. When locating a single alphanumeric attribute—the latest field inspection update date—the tabular list was significantly faster (median 12.05 s
vs. 22.70 s; paired t(15) = −14.16, p < 0.001). For accuracy, the tabular list
yielded a higher descriptive score (93.8% [15/16] vs. 81.2% [13/16]), with an exact McNemar test yielding p = 0.625 (discordant pairs: b = 3, c = 1). While the
accuracy difference was not statistically significant at N = 16, the list format
imposed lower interaction friction. In the map-and-timeline condition, participants clicked markers, parsed dialogs, and navigated multiple visual panes, with
two participants confusing the contract completion deadline with the latest inspection date. This confirms Maceli and Yu’s findings [11]: visual complexity
degrades performance on elementary lookups.

Conclusion

This pilot study established an empirical evaluation comparing a conventional
list-based tabular portal against an integrated map-and-timeline interface. Using
a 20-project benchmark modeled on Lamongan Regency, our findings demonstrate clear task-dependent trade-offs:

List-Based vs. Map-and-Timeline Project Information

15

1. Spatial Scoping: Geospatial choropleth maps significantly accelerate geographic retrieval by 57% (p < 0.001), accompanied by a descriptive accuracy
gain from 81.2% to 100.0% (p = 0.250).
2. Schedule Comprehension: Synchronized dual-bar timelines significantly
reduce completion time (p < 0.001) and statistically elevate delay detection
accuracy from 50.0% to 93.8% (p = 0.016).
3. Complexity Penalty: For discrete alphanumeric lookups, tabular lists remain significantly faster (p < 0.001), with descriptive accuracy favoring the
list format (93.8% vs. 81.2%, p = 0.625).
4. Data Latency Vulnerabilities: Omitted or stale data leads over 30% of
citizens to erroneous inferences, mandating explicit visual staleness indicators.
Boundaries of Claims and Limitations: This study evaluated a pilot cohort
of N = 16 participants across 128 trials; while statistically robust for cognitive
processing metrics, it represents preliminary behavioral evidence. Crucially, information comprehension is an interface usability metric, not causal
proof of corruption reduction or institutional accountability [1, 14].
Civic maps and timelines facilitate cognitive access; converting this access into
systemic accountability depends on administrative responsiveness, legal whistleblower protections, and civic empowerment. Future work will extend testing to
longitudinal civic deployments across East Java, investigating adaptive hybrid
interfaces combining quick-lookup drawers with spatial timeline overlays.
