Abstract. Public infrastructure portals predominantly display civil works
in unstructured tabular lists, obscuring geographic context and inducing cognitive confusion between calendar time elapsed, physical completion, and reporting recency. This pilot study investigates whether an
integrated map-and-timeline interface signiﬁcantly enhances citizen comprehension compared to a list-based format. Utilizing CivicTrack, an infrastructure monitoring platform for Lamongan Regency, Indonesia, we
benchmarked 20 municipal projects across a three-phase empirical evaluation. Study 1 validated baseline data integrity. Study 2 executed a counterbalanced within-subjects crossover experiment (N = 16) across four
core retrieval tasks. Study 3 evaluated user interpretation of stale and
missing progress data. The results reveal a task-dependent dichotomy. Interactive geospatial maps signiﬁcantly accelerated spatial retrieval (median 14.30 s vs. 33.30 s; paired t(15) = 18.48, p < 0.001), accompanied by
a descriptive accuracy gain from 81.2% (13/16) to 100.0% (16/16) (exact
McNemar p = 0.250). For schedule delay detection, chronological timelines with explicit variance cues signiﬁcantly reduced completion time
(median 27.75 s vs. 44.50 s; paired t(15) = 16.79, p < 0.001), elevating
delay identiﬁcation accuracy from 50.0% (8/16) to 93.8% (15/16) (exact McNemar p = 0.016) by mitigating the duration-work equivalence
heuristic. Conversely, for isolated attribute lookup, tabular lists were signiﬁcantly faster (median 12.05 s vs. 22.70 s; paired t(15) = −14.16, p <
0.001), with descriptive accuracy favoring the list format (93.8% vs.
81.2%; exact McNemar p = 0.625), exposing multi-view interaction overhead. In missing data evaluations, 31.2% misattributed missing values to
project cancellation. Civic platforms must couple spatial overviews with
frictionless tabular inspection.
Keywords: Civic Technology · Public Dashboard · Geospatial Visualization · Usability Evaluation · Open Data.

Introduction

Civic engagement in public infrastructure governance has emerged as an indispensable cornerstone of transparent, accountable, and participatory regional development [2, 6]. Across decentralized administrations and emerging economies,

municipal governments allocate substantial capital budgets to critical public
works—such as road resurfacing, ﬂood mitigation canals, bridge renewals, and
primary healthcare centers [12]. Ordinary citizens have a vital and immediate
stake in these civil works, requiring accessible information to navigate daily commuting routes, evaluate public service delivery, and monitor whether municipal
tax expenditures translate into tangible community assets [13,16]. However, citizens seeking to discover local projects and track their execution progress confront
pervasive information barriers [2, 13].
Similar to patterns documented in municipal e-government and open data
portals internationally [12, 16], statutory disclosures in regional administrations
such as Lamongan Regency, East Java—published via municipal e-procurement
portals (LPSE Kabupaten Lamongan) and open data repositories (Satu Data
Lamongan)—likewise sequester public works data in static tabular registries,
fragmented spreadsheets, or dense PDF tender documents. These legacy tabular
formats strip away geographic context and spatial proximity, forcing citizens
to manually sift through hundreds of uncoordinated textual rows without an
intuitive means to discern which construction projects impact their immediate
neighborhood or whether work has legitimately commenced on the ground.
While open government portals are widely documented to suﬀer from usability barriers and cognitive friction that hinder citizen evaluation [13, 16], a more
acute operational bottleneck identiﬁed during our preliminary ﬁeld observations
in Lamongan is the prevalent conﬂation among three distinct dimensions: physical milestone progress, calendar duration elapsed, and ﬁeld reporting recency.
Preliminary ﬁeld observations conducted between August and September 2024
involved 12 civic community members and local residents in Lamongan examining municipal public works information on LPSE Kabupaten Lamongan and
Satu Data Lamongan, the two primary oﬃcial disclosure platforms referenced
above. These observations were conducted as semi-structured, task-based sessions, in which participants were asked to locate and interpret information on
a speciﬁc ongoing project while researchers recorded their verbal reasoning and
navigation behavior. These sessions revealed two severe cognitive breakdowns:
1. Duration-as-Progress Fallacy: Nine out of 12 participants erroneously
assumed that elapsed calendar time represented physical progress. When inspecting an ongoing road project showing “75% elapsed duration” alongside
“60% physical progress,” participants intuitively concluded that the project
was on track, failing to recognize a critical 15% execution delay—an intuitive
error reﬂecting cognitive substitution heuristics under mismatched tabular
representations [4].
2. Ambiguity over Stale and Missing Data: Oﬃcial disclosures lacked
visual freshness indicators. When progress updates lagged by 3–5 months,
citizens could not discern whether projects were actively ongoing, quietly
halted, or completed; missing progress ﬁelds were frequently assumed to
denote project cancellation or 0% work, mirroring negative default inferences
documented in visualization research on missing data [7].

To overcome the communicative limitations of raw spreadsheets, municipal
agencies and smart city platforms have increasingly transitioned toward visual
dashboards featuring interactive geographic maps and graphical charts [10, 15].
Nonetheless, existing civic dashboard implementations have predominantly been
driven by superﬁcial interface aesthetics (“dashboard gloss”) rather than rigorous human-computer interaction (HCI) evaluation grounded in user cognitive
comprehension tasks [6]. Current literature in open government data frequently
evaluates portals through technical interoperability, metadata completeness, or
general citizen satisfaction surveys, largely ignoring how speciﬁc visual representations inﬂuence the cognitive speed and accuracy of non-expert citizens during
actual information processing [11, 13]. Deploying rich cartographic layers and
visual widgets without task-based empirical grounding introduces substantial
cognitive trade-oﬀs: while interactive maps excel at synoptic spatial orientation,
they introduce interaction friction—such as panning, zooming, marker occlusion, and visual clutter—that actively degrades performance on simple, discrete
attribute lookups [8]. Consequently, uncalibrated visual complexity risks disorienting citizens rather than empowering them.
This gap—the absence of task-based empirical evidence on how visual representation aﬀects citizen cognitive performance—motivates the present study.
To address it, this paper proposes and evaluates CivicTrack, an integrated municipal monitoring platform designed around Cognitive Fit Theory [4, 5]. CivicTrack introduces a synchronized multi-representation architecture that harmonizes an interactive geospatial map (for spatial boundary and cluster scoping), a
dual-progress chronological timeline visualizer (explicitly decoupling contractual
duration from physical milestone progress with clear reporting recency indicators), and a structured, ﬁlterable list view (for direct attribute retrieval). We
investigate the central research question: Does an integrated map-and-timeline
interface signiﬁcantly improve citizen accuracy and eﬃciency in understanding
project locations, physical progress, and schedule delays compared to a conventional list-based presentation?
Utilizing a controlled benchmark dataset of 20 realistic public infrastructure
projects modeled on veriﬁed municipal capital works in Lamongan Regency, this
study establishes three primary contributions:

Related Work

The empirical study of public project dashboard comprehension intersects four
major domains of research: open government data usability, cognitive ﬁt and visual taxonomies, geospatial visualization in civic decision-making, and temporal
project progress modeling under missing or delayed disclosures.
2.1

Public Project Dashboards and Citizen Comprehension

Digital transparency portals are widely heralded to mitigate information asymmetry between public agencies and citizens [2,6,9]. However, reviews and empirical audits show that raw disclosures fail to provide civic utility unless supported
by task-tailored analytical interfaces [2, 12]. Nikiforova and McBride [13] observed that most portals remain passive “data dumps” of static tables that fail
to support non-expert inquiry. While interactive dashboards can enhance comprehension over static displays, complex layouts risk visual fragmentation and
cognitive fatigue [6, 9, 16].
2.2

Cognitive Fit Theory and Visualization Usability

The theoretical foundation explaining how representation formats interact with
task types is grounded in Cognitive Fit Theory, recently extended for data visualization by Bina et al. [5] and evaluated in multi-view settings by Bačić and
Henry [4]. Performance is optimized when information format matches the cognitive task: spatial tasks beneﬁt from perceptual graphical displays, whereas discrete lookups are best served by structured alphanumeric tables [5,15]. However,
combining visual modalities incurs cognitive switching costs when coordinating
views [15, 17]. Standardized usability evaluation protocols provide validated instruments to quantify such interaction friction.
2.3

Geospatial Visualization versus Tabular List Presentations

Cartographic and GIS research categorizes visual analysis into elementary tasks
(locating discrete attributes of known entities) and synoptic tasks (discerning
spatial clusters and distributions) [10,11]. Spatial displays excel at synoptic synthesis, whereas structured tables minimize retrieval costs for discrete attribute
extraction by avoiding map navigation friction (panning, zooming, and marker
occlusion). In participatory urban governance, empirical studies conﬁrm that
while spatial maps facilitate geographic exploration, non-expert citizens struggle when complex thematic layers create visual competition [8, 10, 11].
2.4

Timeline Usability and Schedule Representation

Visualizing chronological project progress requires representing durations, dependencies, and temporal milestones. Human working memory struggles to reconstruct temporal overlaps and pacing from raw alphanumeric date columns

in spreadsheets [15]. In project scheduling, Ayalew and Ayalew [3] evaluated
deterministic and fuzzy Earned Value Management (EVM) methods for public
infrastructure projects, underscoring that measuring physical progress against
planned schedule baselines requires explicit variance tracking rather than simplistic duration comparisons.
2.5

Interpretation of Missing and Stale Governance Data

A persistent challenge in public infrastructure monitoring is data latency and
reporting gaps. Visual displays that omit explicit visual indicators for missing
data frequently lead lay citizens to make negative default inferences, interpreting missing data as administrative failure or canceled works [7]. In civil infrastructure governance, Ambituuni [1] and Saelawong et al. [14] investigated the
Infrastructure Transparency Initiative (CoST) through principal-agent theory,
emphasizing that publishing open data is an institutional enabler rather than
an automatic remedy; without reliable, timely data, public disclosures fail to
generate accountability.
2.6

Research Gap and Positioning

Collectively, prior work treats these three representational paradigms in isolation: dashboard usability studies [2,6,12,13] evaluate aggregate interface comprehension without decomposing task type; map-versus-table studies [10, 11] quantify spatial-versus-tabular trade-oﬀs but omit temporal schedule reasoning; and
timeline visualization research [3, 15] addresses duration representation without
linking it to spatial or tabular retrieval. No prior study empirically compares
all three representations—map, timeline, and table—within a single counterbalanced task battery on real municipal infrastructure data, nor explicitly measures
how citizens misinterpret stale or missing progress ﬁelds. This paper addresses
that gap by integrating Cognitive Fit Theory [4, 5] with a task-diﬀerentiated,
within-subjects evaluation of CivicTrack.

Methodology

3.1

Research Question and Diﬀerentiated Hypotheses

This study investigates the following primary research question: Does an integrated map-and-timeline interface improve citizen accuracy and eﬃciency in
understanding project locations, physical progress, and schedule delays compared
to a conventional list-based presentation?
Because completion time (continuous ratio variable, seconds) and comprehension accuracy (paired binary outcome, 0/1) represent distinct operational
dimensions evaluated under separate statistical tests (paired t-tests and exact
McNemar tests, respectively), we formulate separated operational hypotheses:

– H1 (Spatial Scoping): For spatial discovery tasks, the map interface will
achieve signiﬁcantly shorter completion times (H1a ) and higher descriptive
or statistical retrieval accuracy (H1b ) by eliminating serial scanning fatigue.
– H2 (Schedule Pacing): For schedule delay detection, the dual-bar timeline
interface with visual variance cues will yield signiﬁcantly faster completion
times (H2a ) and signiﬁcantly higher delay identiﬁcation accuracy (H2b ) by
mitigating the duration-work equivalence heuristic.
– H3 (Discrete Lookups): For discrete metadata lookups (latest ﬁeld update
date), the tabular list will achieve signiﬁcantly faster response times (H3a )
and superior descriptive accuracy (H3b ) due to the absence of multi-view
spatial navigation overhead.

The empirical evaluation was conducted using CivicTrack, an end-to-end municipal infrastructure monitoring platform engineered for the 27 subdistricts

responsive CSS, integrating Leaﬂet.js for vector boundary rendering and marker
clustering; and (3) Analytical Engine calculating elapsed duration, milestoneweighted physical completion, and schedule variance (∆). Figure 1 illustrates the

Stimulus Design and Feature Parity Analysis

implemented with identical underlying project data, illustrated in Fig. 2:

Fig. 2. Visual stimuli for comparative evaluation: (a) Condition A (Tabular List baseline modeled on municipal open data portals) and (b) Condition B (CivicTrack interactive Map-and-Timeline Explorer).

2. Condition B (Map-and-Timeline Explorer): An integrated dual-pane

uniform “In-Progress” badges. This deliberate distinction mirrors real-world egovernment conditions and directly evaluates whether citizens can detect delays
when relying solely on numerical columns versus explicit graphical variance cues.

Study 2: Controlled Comprehension Experiment (Pilot Study): Sixteen
participants (N = 16; 8 male, 8 female; aged 21–29 years, M = 24.2, SD = 2.6)

4.2

Comparative Task Performance Analysis (Study 2)

The quantitative outcomes of the 128 trial runs (16 participants × 4 tasks ×
2 conditions) are synthesized in Table 2 and illustrated in Fig. 3.
Spatial Processing vs. Tabular Scanning (Task 1): Task 1 strongly corroborated hypothesis H1a : the interactive map signiﬁcantly accelerated spatial
project retrieval by 57% (median 14.30 s vs. 33.30 s; paired t(15) = 18.48, p <
0.001). For retrieval accuracy (H1b ), Condition B achieved a descriptive gain
from 81.2% (13/16) to 100.0% (16/16). Because accuracy represents paired binary data, an exact two-sided McNemar test was evaluated across discordant
pairs (b = 0, c = 3), yielding p = 0.250. While choropleth visual clustering
eliminated serial scanning slips, the accuracy increase remains a descriptive improvement and does not reach statistical signiﬁcance within this pilot cohort
(N = 16).
Mitigation of Schedule Delay Heuristics (Tasks 2 and 4): Tasks 2 and 4
supported hypotheses H2a and H2b , showing statistically veriﬁed gains in both
completion time and comprehension accuracy. For task eﬃciency (H2a ), the dualbar timeline signiﬁcantly cut completion duration by 15–17 s (Task 2: paired
t(15) = 16.79, p < 0.001; Task 4: paired t(15) = 16.82, p < 0.001). Crucially, the
paired binary accuracy gains were statistically conﬁrmed via exact McNemar

tests (H2b ): delay detection accuracy rose signiﬁcantly from 50.0% (8/16) to

Interface Complexity Overhead in Elementary Lookups (Task 3): Importantly, Task 3 corroborated H3a , exposing the operational tax of spatial visual
interfaces. When locating a single alphanumeric attribute—the latest ﬁeld inspection update date—the tabular list was signiﬁcantly faster (median 12.05 s
vs. 22.70 s; paired t(15) = −14.16, p < 0.001). For accuracy, the tabular list
yielded a higher descriptive score (93.8% [15/16] vs. 81.2% [13/16]), with an exact McNemar test yielding p = 0.625 (discordant pairs: b = 3, c = 1). While the
accuracy diﬀerence was not statistically signiﬁcant at N = 16, the list format imposed lower interaction friction. In the map-and-timeline condition, participants

5

Conclusion

This pilot study established an empirical evaluation comparing a conventional
list-based tabular portal against an integrated map-and-timeline interface. Using
a 20-project benchmark modeled on Lamongan Regency, our ﬁndings demonstrate clear task-dependent trade-oﬀs:
1. Spatial Scoping: Geospatial choropleth maps signiﬁcantly accelerate geographic retrieval by 57% (p < 0.001), accompanied by a descriptive accuracy
gain from 81.2% to 100.0% (p = 0.250).
2. Schedule Comprehension: Synchronized dual-bar timelines with explicit
variance cues signiﬁcantly reduce completion time (p < 0.001) and statistically elevate delay detection accuracy from 50.0% to 93.8% (p = 0.016).
3. Complexity Penalty: For discrete alphanumeric lookups, tabular lists remain signiﬁcantly faster (p < 0.001), with descriptive accuracy favoring the
list format (93.8% vs. 81.2%, p = 0.625).
4. Data Latency Vulnerabilities: Omitted or stale data leads over 30% of
citizens to erroneous inferences, mandating explicit visual staleness indicators.
Boundaries of Claims and Limitations: This study evaluated a pilot cohort
of N = 16 participants across 128 trials; while statistically robust for cognitive
processing metrics, it represents preliminary behavioral evidence. Furthermore,
because Condition B paired graphical timelines with explicit delay badges (“Terlambat”) while Condition A used uniform “In-Progress” labels, observed gains
